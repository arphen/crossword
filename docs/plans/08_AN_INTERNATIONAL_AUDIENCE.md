# An international crossword needs a situated audience

Concept note, 27 September 2026. Part of the [conceptual correspondence](README.md#conceptual-correspondence).

“General” and “concrete” pull in different directions in a useful way. A crossword can range widely over knowledge, but it always addresses someone with particular languages, experiences, and expectations. There is no culturally empty vocabulary from which an international puzzle can be assembled. Removing local references eventually removes texture as well as obstacles.

The opportunity is to make several cultural starting points viable within a shared editorial form.

## Separate the standard from the presumed reader

I would unpack “NYT-grade” into an editorial ambition and an audience assumption. The ambition includes economical clues, lively phrases, controlled ambiguity, fair crossings, satisfying themes, and a trustworthy relationship with the constructor. Those are useful aspirations. The particular background knowledge presumed by any publication is a separate choice.

Your examples—American sports, awards, Broadway, Jewish cultural references—identify material that can be disproportionately familiar to some readers. But they should not become one undifferentiated bucket of “American stuff.” Jewish languages, histories, religious traditions, and everyday vocabularies extend far beyond the United States; Broadway itself connects to an international theatrical culture. The precise editorial problem is presumed familiarity with a particular reference, not the presence of a whole cultural tradition.

A clue can include something unfamiliar and still welcome the player. It can also use an internationally recognizable answer in a way that assumes very local knowledge. The unit that succeeds or fails is often the answer together with the route into it.

## International is not a synonym for universally known

There are at least three useful ambitions here: widely recognizable material; locally meaningful material made accessible through clues and crossings; and personal expertise that gives an individual unusually strong access. These can coexist in one puzzle.

Replacing American sports with European classical music would change who is comfortable without solving the underlying problem. Likewise, an “educated international audience” can quietly become a particular university-educated, English-speaking class. Cooking, repair, gardening, games, popular music, and practical work deserve to count as knowledge alongside theoretical physics.

The *studium generale* becomes much more compelling if every kind of developed attention can purchase passage into unfamiliar subjects. A gardener should get the same structural dignity from knowing a plant that you get from knowing a theorist.

## English competence is a separate dimension

An international English-language crossword faces a difficulty that adding world names cannot solve. A reader may know the science and history but have less immediate access to idioms, conversational fragments, sound-based jokes, or alternate senses of common English words. Those are exactly the resources many elegant clues exploit.

A multilingual scientist may find NOETHER straightforward and a very ordinary English utterance elusive. Calling the latter “easy” conceals the actual demand. Difficulty depends on factual familiarity, language register, clue convention, and the help already supplied by the grid.

Translation is therefore more than exchanging words. A bilingual player may know an entity under one spelling, a concept under another language's label, and an idiom with no natural English equivalent. The answer's intended form must be stable even while the clue recognizes multiple routes to it. This is also why arbitrary transliteration at an unsupported crossing can spoil an otherwise generous puzzle.

## Hospitality does not require cultural flattening

A useful test is whether unfamiliarity feels like an invitation or a membership test. A clue that provides an intelligible description, together with decisive crossings, can let someone encounter an unfamiliar tradition with pleasure. A clue that effectively says “you remember the minor character, surely” demands prior belonging.

Personalization makes this more interesting: different players can reach equally ambitious puzzles through different doors. Fairness need not mean identical experiences. It can mean comparable opportunities to begin, recover, and understand why an answer was right.

Still, a personal crossword should retain some common world. If every reference is tailored away from what the player does not already know, the international promise contracts into a private enclosure. A good editorial voice can say both “I know where you are coming from” and “there is something here you have not met yet.”

## Dialectical postscript

Added after the critical reading in [note 13](15_THE_SELF_CRITIQUE_OF_THE_CONCEPT.md#08-hospitality-without-a-consent-mechanism). This section changes the note.

The note's hospitality test — an intelligible description plus decisive crossings rather than “you remember the minor character, surely” — is a real distinction with no tribunal, and the note's own argument explains why none can be found. Any operationalization of “widely recognizable” reinstates the educated, English-speaking class the note refuses; the test therefore currently rests on the writer's sensibility, which is a criterion in disguise as a faculty.

The determinate gain is a change of locus, and the note already holds the premise: if the unit that succeeds or fails is the answer *together with its route*, then **the international is carried by the crossing structure, not by the roster.** A player's access to an unfamiliar answer is a function of how many of its crossings they independently hold, which is computable per player without consulting any canon, and which separates the note's own cases without appeal to taste. NOETHER is generous because the science-side crossings belong to the solver; the minor-character clue fails because its crossings are nothing but the reference. This supplies the missing criterion and reorders the note's priorities: crossing density and placement become editorial instruments in their own right, not consequences of answer choice, and a puzzle can be international by construction while remaining locally specific in every answer it contains.

Two additions follow. **Consent:** an unfamiliar element is not only an offer but an imposition at the moment of the player's greatest exposure, so it must be refusable, and the refusal must move the *pack* rather than the fact — “not for me” is evidence about a symbolic order, which this note insists cannot be surveyed from outside; a profile that cannot register rejection can only register capture. **Dose:** strangeness is welcome only where an interest has already been constituted, and interest is built by work already done, so hospitality is a property of sequence rather than of clue quality. Four earlier puzzles that gave a solver reasons to care about opera can make one opera clue a pleasure; no clue, however well written, can do it cold. That converts this note's “comparable opportunities to begin” from a hope into something schedulable.

What this changes: compute and log crossing-availability per answer per player as the hospitality measure; add a refusal affordance with pack-level rather than entity-level effect; and treat the proportion of foreign elements per puzzle as a dose to be titrated across a week rather than a quality to be maximized in a day.
