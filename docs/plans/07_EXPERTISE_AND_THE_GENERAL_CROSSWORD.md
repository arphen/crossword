# Expertise as a way into the world

Concept note, 27 September 2026. Part of the [conceptual correspondence](README.md#conceptual-correspondence); an argument about the experience, not an implementation specification.

Your physics example contains the most consequential product idea in the whole prompt: a crossword lets uneven knowledge become useful beyond its own territory. Knowing a theorist's name buys letters in an unfamiliar poem, food, or city. The pleasure is not merely recognition. It is discovering that something you know can get you somewhere you did not know how to enter.

That makes the crossword an unusually good form for a *studium generale*. Its subjects are structurally interdependent. In a trivia quiz, ignorance in one category stays there. In a crossword, competence travels. Someone does not have to become a generalist before they can enjoy a generalist experience; the grid lends them the necessary connections.

## The opening repertoire analogy

The e4/d4 comparison is productive because an opening repertoire gives a player familiar positions from which genuinely unfamiliar play can develop. A physics specialist does not need a physics-only crossword any more than an e4 player needs every game to repeat the same opening line. They need enough recognizable structure to begin making consequential decisions.

But there is an important qualification: knowing physicists and knowing physics are overlapping, different competences. A theorist might retrieve an equation immediately and forget its historical attribution. An enthusiast might know every famous surname without understanding the mathematics. Even your own “physics expertise” is likely a particular shape rather than a uniformly high score across a category.

This suggests a richer meaning of a personal foothold: an answer the player can plausibly retrieve through the particular route offered by its clue. An accessible name under an obscure biographical clue stops being an opening. A familiar concept under an elegant everyday clue may become one.

## A seed has value through what it opens

Suppose DIRAC is immediately available to you. That is a satisfying five-letter recognition. Its larger value depends on where those five letters land. Do they distinguish two plausible answers? Establish the start of an unfamiliar surname? Let a phrase suddenly become readable? Or disappear into a region whose other clues all require equally specialized physics knowledge?

The same personal entry can therefore be generous or inert. Personal relevance is partly a property of its placement and its neighbors.

This is where selecting “ten things the user likes” is conceptually inadequate. Ten familiar answers can cluster in one corner while another corner remains inaccessible. Conversely, a few carefully distributed footholds can make an otherwise demanding puzzle feel hospitable. A useful editorial question is: **where can this player's knowledge go next?**

There is also a difference between mechanical support and meaningful discovery. Completing an unknown name entirely through crossings can be fair and occasionally necessary. But it need not teach anything memorable. The clue should leave the newly completed answer attached to some intelligible distinction, image, or fact. Otherwise the grid has transported letters without transporting understanding.

## Expertise should sometimes be unsettled

Expertise supplies more than easy entries. It supplies strong expectations, and strong expectations make sophisticated misdirection possible. A physicist meeting FIELD, CHARGE, SPIN, or CHARM may initially privilege the technical sense. An everyday reading can then become the surprise. The specialist can be both advantaged by knowledge and playfully caught by it.

That is a more interesting form of personalization than relentlessly affirming competence. It acknowledges the player's habits of attention and occasionally lets another meaning interrupt them. The joke must remain recoverable: the expert should enjoy having taken the bait, not feel punished for knowing the subject.

This also argues for different kinds of personal material. Names provide recognition; technical concepts provide competence; ordinary words shared with a discipline provide semantic movement. A physics pack composed entirely of famous men would miss much of what makes physics an interesting language to think through.

## The experience worth aiming at

The strongest version of the product lets a player feel, “My knowledge belongs here, and it is sufficient to begin.” It then gives that knowledge consequences outside its home territory.

There is no need for every puzzle to traverse every discipline. The generality can unfold across many sessions. What matters locally is the alternation between recognition, uncertainty, and earned discovery. The player's specialty is a departure point. The editorial achievement is making the journey worth taking.

## Dialectical postscript

Added after the critical reading in [note 13](15_THE_SELF_CRITIQUE_OF_THE_CONCEPT.md#07-the-foothold-has-no-zero-position). This section changes the note; it is not a summary of the critique.

This note has a concept of the foothold and no concept of its opposite, and the omission costs it its central claim. The opposite is not the hard answer but the **unmarked cell**: a crossing solved without being met, a square whose only function was to permit. That category is already present here, in the sentence distinguishing mechanical support from meaningful discovery, and it is the strongest thing in the note, because it implies that a crossword's experienced content is a *ratio and distribution* rather than a sum of recognitions. Thirty met answers and forty passed through are not “a bit better” than the reverse arrangement; they are a different object, and no version of this note's argument can tell them apart. The measurable consequence is immediate and belongs to the solve record, not to the generator: for each answer, was its clue opened at all before the letters arrived? A puzzle that answers “no” for two-thirds of its entries has converted personalization into letters, which is the failure mode this note was written to avoid.

Second, the *studium generale* has to be sublated rather than pursued. Generality as topical coverage is an abstraction: no fifteen-by-fifteen grid contains the disciplines, and every entry chosen for one expertise is a refusal of another, which makes the note's optimism about traveling competence partly an illusion about budgets. What survives is not coverage but the **specimen** — one entry whose crossings are chosen so that the grid's dependence structure becomes visible: where this player's knowledge enters, what it buys, what remains strictly unreachable without it, and where a stranger is carried through. Generality then becomes a property of a single realized puzzle instead of a policy over many, which is the only form of it a solver can actually experience.

Third, the note asks only what a foothold opens and never what it **forecloses**. An entry's negative job — what it makes impossible, and therefore what the puzzle is visibly *for* — is what turns rationing from a statistical fact into an editorial one, and it is the missing half of the distribution argument in the section on selecting ten liked things.

Fourth, the opening-repertoire analogy conceals a producer. To bait a physicist with an everyday reading of FIELD, CHARGE, or SPIN requires someone who can hold a discipline's ambiguity open while writing, which is expertise plus wit plus restraint, and this note's economics are entirely demand-side. The trap is not a property of the word or of the player; it is placed, and the placing is the product.

What this changes: add the clue-before-letters measure to the evidence model; score candidate entries for bridge value and for negative job, not only for accessibility; treat one puzzle as the unit of generality; and describe the constructor's required capacities with the same care this note gives the player's.
