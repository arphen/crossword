Telemetry-Driven Cognitive Modeling and Procedural Generation in Adaptive Educational CrosswordsDigital crossword puzzles have evolved from static leisure activities into dynamic, constraint-governed learning environments capable of continuous psychometric assessment and personalized vocabulary instruction. Unlike unconstrained retrieval settings such as flashcards or multiple-choice questions, crossword puzzle solving is embedded in an interconnected lattice of orthographic and semantic constraints. Every character entered at coordinate cell $(r, c)$ simultaneously restricts the candidate space of two intersecting lexical entries. Modeling learner cognition in this domain requires disentangling autonomous lexical recall from letter-guided recognition, opportunistic guessing, and phonotactic deduction.Telemetry and Cognitive Signal ExtractionModern web-based puzzle interfaces serve as high-frequency behavioral instrumentation layers. By logging client-side event streams—including millisecond-resolution keypresses, cursor coordinates, input focus shifts, and clue inspections—systems can infer a solver's latent cognitive and metacognitive states during problem-solving.Cognitive Significance of Interface TelemetryThe diagnostic utility of crossword telemetry relies on parsing how a learner interacts with both the physical constraints of the grid and the linguistic content of the clues.The sequence in which a learner attempts and completes words reveals their strategic approach and retrieval autonomy. When a learner solves an entry on a completely empty slot ($k = 0$, where $k$ represents the number of intersecting letters already placed), they demonstrate unassisted cued recall. The learner's internal lexicon maps directly from the semantic representation of the clue and the length constraint $L$ to the target lexical entry. Conversely, when an entry is solved only after one or more crossing characters have been placed ($k \ge 1$), the cognitive task shifts toward orthographically constrained recognition or cued lexical search. Tracking the ratio of unconstrained to constrained solutions across a session reveals the extent to which a learner relies on environmental scaffolding versus independent semantic memory.Keystroke dynamics provide a fine-grained, continuous measure of cognitive load and lexical retrieval fluency. Dwell time and time-to-first-keystroke (TTFK)—the latency between focusing on a clue or grid slot and depressing the first key—measure the time required for clue comprehension, lexical search, and initial orthographic planning. A brief TTFK followed by rapid entry indicates immediate, automated lexical access. In contrast, an extended TTFK indicates prolonged semantic activation, an extensive search through competing candidates, or difficulties in clue interpretation.Inter-keystroke intervals (IKIs) measure the time between successive keypresses within an entry. Fluid lexical retrieval exhibits short, regular IKIs (typically 120 to 250 milliseconds), reflecting motor execution of an already retrieved orthographic string. Significant pauses between syllables or morphemes indicate cognitive chunking, intermediate spell-checking, or phonotactic assembly.Key hold duration—the interval between keydown and keyup events—reflects motor tension and decision certainty. Frequent backspacing, deletions, and overwrites signal lexical competition and error monitoring. A single backspace immediately following a keystroke typically indicates a simple motor typo, whereas clearing an entire entry reveals structural disconfirmation from crossing clues or a realization that the selected word does not match the clue's semantic frame.A solver's navigation patterns across the grid reveal their executive control and problem-solving strategy. Sequential scanning—proceeding linearly through across and down clues in numerical order—is common among novices who lack flexible heuristic strategies, as well as high-expertise solvers who can answer clues without structural help. In contrast, opportunistic "hop-and-seek" navigation involves jumping across the grid to prioritize slots where newly filled intersections have reduced the search space. This pattern aligns with optimal information-foraging behavior by minimizing candidate entropy.Differentiating active from passive clue engagement provides further diagnostic nuance. Active engagement is characterized by selecting a slot and attempting input, whereas passive engagement consists of scrolling through clues or lingering over slots without typing. In educational crosswords, the grid lattice directly violates this assumption: solving an across word places letters in multiple intersecting down slots, altering their effective difficulty and item-level conditional dependencies.Adapting Psychometric Models to Graph-Entangled AssessmentsTo account for local item dependencies, the puzzle grid is modeled as an undirected bipartite graph $G = (V_C, V_E, E)$, where $V_C$ represents the set of character cells $(r, c)$, $V_E$ is the set of lexical entries, and edges represent character containment.Testlet Response Theory (TRT), developed by Wainer, Bradlow, and Wang, provides a formal approach for modeling clustered item dependencies. In a crossword puzzle, intersecting word clusters or local grid sectors are treated as dynamic testlets. Under a two-parameter logistic testlet model (2PL-TRT), the probability that learner $j$ correctly answers entry $i$ within an intersecting cluster $d(i)$ is formulated as:$$P(Y_{ij} = 1 \mid \theta_j, \alpha_i, \beta_i, \gamma_{jd(i)}) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(-\alpha_i \left[ \theta_j - \beta_i - \gamma_{jd(i)} \right]\right)}$$where $\theta_j$ is the learner's latent vocabulary ability, $\alpha_i$ is the discrimination parameter of entry $i$, $\beta_i$ is its baseline difficulty, and $\gamma_{jd(i)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{d(i)}^2)$ is a random testlet effect that captures shared local variance, such as letter leakage or overlapping domain knowledge.Because intersecting characters progressively constrain the search space, an entry's difficulty must be parameterized dynamically. Let $L_i$ be the total length of entry $i$, and let $k_i \in \{0, \dots, L_i-1\}$ be the number of intersecting letters already placed when the learner attempts the entry. The dynamic difficulty parameter $\beta_i(k_i, \mathbf{c}_i)$ is defined as:$$\beta_i(k_i, \mathbf{c}_i) = \beta_{i, 0} \left(1 - \frac{k_i}{L_i}\right)^\eta - \sum_{m \in \mathcal{K}_i} I(c_m, m)$$where $\beta_{i, 0}$ is the pristine, unassisted item difficulty; $\eta \ge 1$ is an empirical parameter modeling non-linear returns from scaffolding; $\mathcal{K}_i$ is the set of revealed letter indices; and $I(c_m, m)$ represents the information entropy reduction provided by character $c_m$ at position $m$ within word $i$. The information contribution relative to the dictionary of length-matched words $\mathcal{D}_{L_i}$ is given by:$$I(c_m, m) = -\log_2 \frac{\vert{}\{w \in \mathcal{D}_{L_i} : w[m] = c_m\}\vert{}}{\vert{}\mathcal{D}_{L_i}\vert{}}$$Accounting for pseudoguessing as the candidate space shrinks, the dynamic response probability is parameterized as a 3PL model:$$P(Y_i = 1 \mid \theta, \beta_{i, 0}, k_i) = c(k_i) + \frac{1 - c(k_i) - s}{1 + \exp\left(-\alpha_i \left[\theta - \beta_i(k_i, \mathbf{c}_i)\right]\right)}$$where $s$ is the operational slip probability, and $c(k_i)$ is the dynamic guessing floor:$$c(k_i) = \min\left(1.0, \, \frac{1}{\max\left(1, \, \vert{}\mathcal{D}_{\text{pattern}}(\mathbf{c}_i)\vert{}\right)}\right)$$in which $\mathcal{D}_{\text{pattern}}(\mathbf{c}_i)$ represents the set of dictionary words that match the revealed intersecting characters.Classical Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (BKT) updates skill mastery $L_t$ using four parameters: prior knowledge $P(L_0)$, transition probability $P(T)$, guess probability $P(G)$, and slip probability $P(S)$. In crossword environments, the contextual guess-and-slip framework introduced by Baker et al. can be extended to dynamically estimate $P(G)$ and $P(S)$ from telemetry and structural scaffolding:$$P(G_t) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(-\left[w_0 + w_1 \frac{k_i}{L_i} - w_2 \text{TTFK}_t + w_3 \log \vert{}\mathcal{D}_{\text{pattern}}\vert{}^{-1}\right]\right)}$$$$P(G_t) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\left(-\left[w_0 + w_1 \frac{k_i}{L_i} - w_2 \text{TTFK}_t + w_3 \log \vert{}\mathcal{D}_{\text{pattern}}\vert{}^{-1}\right]\right)}$$When an entry is completed with a high scaffold ratio ($k_i/L_i$) and irregular keystroke intervals, $P(G_t)$ increases, preventing an unearned upward update to the latent mastery state $P(L_t \mid \text{Observation}_t)$.For neural approaches such as Deep Knowledge Tracing (DKT), standard sequence models struggle because grid solving is non-linear. Graph-augmented Deep Knowledge Tracing (G-DKT) addresses this by coupling an LSTM with a Graph Convolutional Network (GCN):$$\mathbf{h}_t = \text{LSTM}\left( [\mathbf{e}_{q_t} \oplus a_t \oplus \mathbf{g}_t], \, \mathbf{h}_{t-1} \right)$$where $\mathbf{e}_{q_t}$ is the semantic embedding of the target clue-word pair, $a_t \in \{0, 1\}$ is the accuracy score adjusted for fluency, and $\mathbf{g}_t$ is an embedding vector derived from the GCN over the grid lattice $G$. The GCN propagates character certainty marginals across intersecting nodes, reflecting how solving one clue clarifies adjacent, intersecting entries.Integrating Spaced Repetition into Continuous Generation PipelinesSpaced repetition systems (SRS) evaluate memory decay to determine optimal review timing. from typing import List, Dict, Optional, Tuple

class LearnerMemoryState:
    def __init__(self, difficulty: float, stability: float, last_review_timestamp: float):
        self.D = difficulty  # Inherent word difficulty: 1.0 to 10.0
        self.S = stability   # Memory stability in days
        self.last_review = last_review_timestamp

    def compute_retrievability(self, current_time: float) -> float:
        delta_days = (current_time - self.last_review) / 86400.0
        decay_factor = (0.9 ** (-1.0 / 0.5)) - 1.0
        return (1.0 + decay_factor * (delta_days / max(0.1, self.S))) ** (-0.5)

class WordCandidate:
    def __init__(self, lemma: str, base_surprisal: float):
        self.lemma = lemma.upper()
        self.length = len(lemma)
        self.base_surprisal = base_surprisal

def compute_effective_surprisal(word: WordCandidate, filled_cross_letters: int) -> float:
    decay_ratio = 1.0 - (float(filled_cross_letters) / float(word.length))
    return word.base_surprisal * max(0.0, decay_ratio)

def score_word_for_slot(
    candidate: WordCandidate,
    slot_id: int,
    filled_cross_letters: int,
    learner_theta: float,
    memory_profile: Dict[str, LearnerMemoryState],
    letter_frequency_index: Dict[Tuple[int, str], int],
    current_time: float,
    weights: Tuple[float, float, float, float] = (0.40, 0.35, 0.15, 0.10)
) -> float:
    w_srs, w_zone, w_afford, w_fatigue = weights
    
    # 1. Spaced Repetition Urgency (Target retention R* = 0.88)
    mem_state = memory_profile.get(candidate.lemma)
    if mem_state:
        retrievability = mem_state.compute_retrievability(current_time)
        s_srs = max(0.0, 0.88 - retrievability)
    else:
        s_srs = 0.25  # Exploration incentive for unencountered vocabulary

    # 2. Zone of Proximal Development Surprisal Alignment
    target_surprisal = 3.0 + 2.0 * learner_theta
    s_eff = compute_effective_surprisal(candidate, filled_cross_letters)
    s_zone = -abs(s_eff - target_surprisal)

    # 3. Intersection Affordance Metric
    s_affordance = 0.0
    for idx, char in enumerate(candidate.lemma):
        branching_factor = letter_frequency_index.get((idx, char), 1)
        s_affordance += math.log(branching_factor + 1)
    s_affordance /= candidate.length

    # 4. Lexical Recency / Fatigue Penalty
    s_fatigue = 0.0
    if mem_state:
        delta_hours = (current_time - mem_state.last_review) / 3600.0
        if delta_hours < 24.0:
            s_fatigue = 1.0 / max(1.0, delta_hours)

    return (w_srs * s_srs) + (w_zone * s_zone) + (w_afford * s_affordance) - (w_fatigue * s_fatigue)

def adaptive_backtracking_search(
    assigned_slots: Dict[int, str],
    unassigned_slots: List[int],
    grid_domains: Dict[int, List[WordCandidate]],
    intersections: Dict[int, List[Tuple[int, int, int]]],  # (neighbor_slot, my_idx, n_idx)
    learner_theta: float,
    memory_profile: Dict[str, LearnerMemoryState],
    letter_index: Dict[Tuple[int, str], int],
    current_time: float
) -> Optional[Dict[int, str]]:
    if not unassigned_slots:
        return assigned_slots

    # Minimum Remaining Values (MRV) heuristic
    unassigned_slots.sort(key=lambda s: len(grid_domains[s]))
    current_slot = unassigned_slots[0]

    crossing_edges = intersections.get(current_slot, [])
    filled_count = sum(1 for (n_slot, _, _) in crossing_edges if n_slot in assigned_slots)

    # Filter and rank valid candidates for the current slot
    scored_candidates = []
    for cand in grid_domains[current_slot]:
        consistent = True
        for (n_slot, my_idx, n_idx) in crossing_edges:
            if n_slot in assigned_slots:
                if assigned_slots[n_slot][n_idx] != cand.lemma[my_idx]:
                    consistent = False
                    break
        if not consistent:
            continue

        cand_score = score_word_for_slot(
            cand, current_slot, filled_count, learner_theta,
            memory_profile, letter_index, current_time
        )
        scored_candidates.append((cand_score, cand))

    # Order domain values by descending pedagogical score
    scored_candidates.sort(key=lambda x: x[0], reverse=True)

    for _, chosen_word in scored_candidates:
        assigned_slots[current_slot] = chosen_word.lemma
        
        # Forward checking: prune inconsistent words from neighbor domains
        pruned_domains: Dict[int, List[WordCandidate]] = {}
        conflict_detected = False
        
        for (n_slot, my_idx, n_idx) in crossing_edges:
            if n_slot not in assigned_slots:
                pruned_domains[n_slot] = [
                    w for w in grid_domains[n_slot]
                    if w.lemma[n_idx] == chosen_word.lemma[my_idx]
                ]
                if not pruned_domains[n_slot]:
                    conflict_detected = True
                    break
        
        if not conflict_detected:
            saved_domains = {s: grid_domains[s] for s in pruned_domains}
            grid_domains.update(pruned_domains)
            
            result = adaptive_backtracking_search(
                assigned_slots,
                [s for s in unassigned_slots if s != current_slot],
                grid_domains,
                intersections,
                learner_theta,
                memory_profile,
                letter_index,
                current_time
            )
            if result is not None:
                return result
            
            grid_domains.update(saved_domains)

        del assigned_slots[current_slot]

    return None
